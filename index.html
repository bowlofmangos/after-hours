In 1994, Argus closed its ninth floor after a night clerk found next week's attendance sheet. Every missing employee had signed it.

The floor still draws power. Payroll still runs. At 2:17 each morning, the employees turn from their desks to inspect the latest arrival.

The boy in the hallway is the clerk's son. He has been singing the same unfinished song for thirty-two years. When he falls silent, something above the ceiling listens. Stay low until he starts again.

Tonight, your name is on the sheet.
Find three amber personnel records. Reach the green exit.
Don't stare back. When the singing stops, hold C to crouch.

1978 / THE EXTRA FLOOR

The original building plans show eight floors. The ninth first appeared on an elevator inspection report, handwritten between the roof and the machinery room. Its offices were already furnished. The coffee was warm. Every desk held a photograph of someone the inspector almost recognized.

1986 / PERSONNEL CONTINUITY

Argus stopped recording resignations. Employees who left received salary deposits anyway. Their replacements reported finding their own belongings in occupied cubicles: childhood lunchboxes, house keys, letters they had never sent. Management described this as a successful transition.

1994 / THE NIGHT CLERK

Clerk Daniel Vale brought his son, Ellis, to work during a school closure. At 2:17 AM, the elevators opened on a corridor that should have ended at the fire stairs. Daniel told Ellis to sing so they could find each other in the dark. Only the boy's voice returned through the corridor. Daniel came back another way: above the ceiling tiles.

“That's not my wife at reception. She knows our address, but she can't remember the color of our front door. Ellis, keep singing. Whatever you hear, don't finish the song.”
— Final recoverable dictation, D. Vale

1995 / THE EMPLOYEES

The people at the desks no longer type. The keyboards were disconnected years ago. They watch visitors because the office needs a witness to decide who belongs here. The longer you meet their eyes, the more details they remember about a life you haven't lived.

2008 / ABOVE THE TILES

A maintenance crew reported footsteps overhead, moving between rooms without crossing the intervening corridor. There is no floor above Personnel. The crew lowered their heads when the singing stopped. All four returned. One recovered microphone captured the thing whispering “Ellis” in Daniel Vale’s voice. The footsteps stopped whenever the boy sang his father’s tune.

RECORD 01 / DANIEL VALE — NIGHT CLERK

STATUS: TRANSFERRED / DESTINATION NOT LISTED

Daniel Vale joined Argus in 1989. His task was simple: count the employees after midnight and report any empty desks. For five years, he reported forty-six. Then he found forty-seven coats in the cloakroom. One was his son's.

On the night of the incident, Daniel discovered that the attendance sheet was not predicting disappearances. It was assigning replacements. Every name he crossed out appeared on a different desk. Every name he added became harder to remember outside the building.

“I tried writing my own name in every box. For a moment, every telephone rang. Then the handwriting changed back. There is one empty line it won't let me touch.”
— Note folded inside Vale's timecard

The last camera image shows Daniel entering a meeting room marked PERSONNEL CONTINUITY. The next frame shows the ceiling bowing upward. His body never crossed the doorway again.

A recovered maintenance tape confirms it: the creature above the tiles is Daniel. The office stretched him through the space between floors, keeping just enough of his mind to recognize his son. He follows the singing because he remembers the journey home. When it stops, he searches for whoever took the boy away.

RECORD 02 / ELLIS VALE — DEPENDENT VISITOR

STATUS: AWAITING COLLECTION BY PARENT

Ellis was eight when his father brought him to the office. His visitor badge expires at 2:18 AM. Every camera on the floor has remained at 2:17 since that night.

He sings a tune Daniel used to hum on the journey home. The recording has no final verse. Audio technicians found that when Ellis sings, Daniel settles above the tiles to listen. When the song stops, his searching brings a slow breath directly overhead. Crouching keeps you beneath his searching gaze.

“I don't know the ending. Dad said he'd teach me in the car. If I learn it by myself, he won't have to come back for me.”
— Interview conducted through the stairwell door, 2008

Sometimes he remembers how long he has been waiting and begins to cry. Comfort him before the crying carries through the vents. He still believes every approaching footstep might be his father’s. He is right. When he cries, Daniel hears distress and comes looking for its cause. Ellis does not know what his father has become.

RECORD 03 / YOU — RETURNING EMPLOYEE

Three records. Three reasons the headcount is wrong. Perhaps the stairwell opens because the office cannot decide which of you is leaving.

FILE 04 / THE HEADCOUNT RECONCILIATION ▸

INTERNAL AUDIT / DO NOT SUBMIT TO PAYROLL

The ninth floor was authorized for forty-six employees. Daniel counted forty-six every night because he counted the people sitting at the desks. The office counted positions instead. A name in its ledger remained employed even after its owner vanished.

When Ellis visited in 1994, the system found a forty-seventh person with no assigned desk. It printed a coat label and opened a personnel entry for him. His visitor badge still said DEPENDENT. The ledger said VACANCY FILLED.

THE CORRECTION

Daniel discovered Ellis's new entry and crossed it out. To keep the total at forty-seven, he wrote his own name in its place. But Daniel already occupied one of the original forty-six positions. The office now had two entries for one father.

Personnel Continuity resolved the duplicate by dividing the assignment. The clerk's original entry stayed at his desk. The replacement entry became an overhead position: a custodian stretched through the ceiling void, ordered to locate the missing dependent. Both entries referred to Daniel. Only one still had a body that could move.

“46 assigned staff + 1 dependent − 1 dependent + 1 duplicate appointment = 47 active positions.
Physical presence is not required for continued employment.”
— Automated reconciliation, 02:17 AM

WHY THE COUNT NEVER FALLS

After that night, Argus stopped removing names. When an employee disappeared, their position stayed open until a visitor could be made to recognize it as their own. That is why the employees stare. Recognition completes the transfer. A visitor who sits down gives the ledger a replacement without changing its total.

You are being assigned to Daniel's original desk. The creature upstairs is still charged against his second entry. The office wants you to accept the first so it can finally call the discrepancy resolved.

WHY THREE RECORDS OPEN THE STAIRS

Daniel's record proves the duplicate appointment. Ellis's proves the supposed employee was a child visiting his father. Yours proves the replacement has not yet accepted employment. Presenting all three forces the exit system to classify you as an unresolved visitor, allowing release through the stairwell.

This fourth file is an auditor's explanation, not a personnel credential. It cannot substitute for any of the three records you collect.

04 / THE ROOM TONE EXPERIMENT

ACOUSTIC SURVEY / FOUR MICROPHONES

Researchers removed Ellis's voice from a recording to isolate the sound above the ceiling. Beneath the footsteps, they found Daniel reciting the names of every employee on the floor.

The names were not spoken in alphabetical order. They were spoken in the order each listener would disappear.

At the end, Daniel said the researcher's name. Then he corrected his pronunciation. Then the recording continued for nine minutes after the tape had run out.

The corrected pronunciation was the one the researcher used only at home.

FILE 05 / HOW DANIEL BECAME THE CEILING ▸

PERSONNEL CONTINUITY / 1994

Daniel thought writing his name over Ellis's would let him take his son's place. It did. But Argus could not assign a man who already had a desk to a second desk. The ledger printed a new department: OVERHEAD SUPERVISION.

The meeting room asked him to sign a transfer. Through its closed door, he could hear Ellis crying. The form promised that a supervisor would always be able to hear his dependent. Daniel signed before reading the line beneath it: COVERAGE MUST EXTEND TO ALL OCCUPIED AREAS.

THE BUILDING MADE ROOM INSIDE HIM

His fingers lengthened across the tabletop. When he tried to stand, his knees bent toward the ceiling. Each time his son called, another joint opened in his shadow. The office was making him large enough to cover every corridor without ever letting him reach the stairs.

The floor-plan marks on his face are the routes he has searched. The extra arms are routes he cannot stop searching. There is no wound to heal. The ledger considers his body correctly configured for the position.

“I accept the transfer. Let him leave.”
— Daniel Vale, final human statement

The office transferred Daniel. It never released Ellis. The boy remained a dependent visitor, waiting for a parent who could no longer fit through the exit.

Some nights Daniel remembers enough to rest a hand beside his son. Other nights he remembers only the instruction written into his new body: FIND THE CAUSE OF DISTRESS.
